# Open-Source Licensing and the Sustainability of Software Libraries

*Keywords: Telkom University, Entrepreneurship, Laboratories*

Open-source software has become the invisible foundation of the digital economy. Operating systems, web servers, databases, programming languages, and countless libraries are developed in public and shared under licenses that grant users broad freedoms. Companies of all sizes depend on these projects, often without realizing how many are embedded in their products. Understanding the legal and practical foundations of open source is therefore essential for anyone who builds, buys, or maintains software today.

A license is the legal instrument that determines what others may do with code. Without one, the default under copyright law is that nobody may copy, modify, or distribute the work. Open-source licenses grant these permissions under defined conditions. Because the conditions differ significantly, choosing a license is not a bureaucratic formality but a strategic decision that affects adoption, contribution, and commercial opportunities. Developers who publish code without selecting a license unintentionally discourage use, since cautious organizations will avoid it.

Permissive licenses, such as MIT and BSD, impose minimal requirements, typically asking only that the original copyright notice be preserved. Users may incorporate the code into proprietary products without sharing their own source. The Apache License 2.0 is similar but adds an explicit grant of patent rights and a clause protecting contributors, which many companies find reassuring. Permissive licenses encourage wide adoption and are popular for libraries intended to become standard building blocks across industries.

Copyleft licenses take a different philosophy. The GNU General Public License requires that derivative works distributed to others also be released under the same license, ensuring that improvements remain open. The Lesser General Public License relaxes this for libraries linked into other programs, while the Affero General Public License extends obligations to software offered over a network. Copyleft protects the commons from being absorbed into closed products, but it also creates compliance considerations that companies must manage carefully.

Compliance is a practical discipline. Organizations should maintain an inventory of the components in their software, often called a software bill of materials, and record the license of each. Automated scanners can detect dependencies and flag incompatible combinations, such as mixing code under licenses with conflicting requirements. Attribution notices must be preserved, and obligations triggered by distribution must be understood. Neglecting these tasks can cause legal disputes, delayed acquisitions, or forced rewrites, all of which are far more expensive than regular review.

The human side of open source is equally important. Many critical libraries are maintained by a handful of volunteers who work unpaid in their spare time. When a serious vulnerability appeared in a widely used encryption library, the world discovered how few people were responsible for infrastructure relied upon by millions. A later flaw in a popular logging library affected enormous numbers of systems almost overnight. These incidents highlight that heavy use does not guarantee adequate support.

Sustainable funding models are therefore a major topic. Some projects receive donations or sponsorships from individuals and companies. Foundations provide neutral governance and pooled resources. Others adopt dual licensing, offering a free open version and a commercial version with different terms, or an open core model with proprietary extensions. Many companies earn revenue by providing hosting, support, training, or consulting around an open project. Each model involves trade-offs between openness, independence, and income that maintainers must consider honestly.

Good governance helps projects survive. Clear contribution guidelines explain how to propose changes, a code of conduct sets expectations for respectful behavior, and defined decision-making processes prevent disputes from paralyzing progress. Succession planning matters as well, since a project dependent on one person is vulnerable if that person burns out or moves on. Communities that welcome newcomers, review contributions promptly, and share responsibility tend to remain healthy for many years.

Documentation and onboarding often determine whether a library thrives. Clear examples, a straightforward installation guide, and honest descriptions of limitations reduce the burden on maintainers by answering common questions in advance. Labeling beginner-friendly issues invites new contributors to participate. Responsible security practices, including a published policy for reporting vulnerabilities and a process for issuing patches, build confidence among users. These investments may seem secondary to writing code, yet they strongly influence a project's reputation and longevity.

When selecting a library, teams should evaluate its health rather than only its popularity. Useful signals include the frequency of recent releases, the responsiveness of maintainers to issues, the number of active contributors, the quality of tests, and the presence of a clear license. A library with a single inactive maintainer may become a liability even if it is widely used today. Having a plan to replace or fork critical dependencies reduces the risk of surprise.

For entrepreneurship, open source is both a resource and a strategy. Startups build on open components to move quickly, and some establish their reputations by releasing useful libraries that attract developers and customers. Businesses built around open projects must think carefully about licensing and community relationships, since a change that appears to betray contributors can provoke strong backlash or forks. Ethical, transparent behavior is an asset that is difficult to rebuild once lost.

[Telkom University](https://it.telkomuniversity.ac.id) can play a constructive role by teaching students to be responsible participants in this ecosystem. Courses can include license analysis, dependency auditing, and contribution workflows, while entrepreneurship programs can explore how open-source ventures create value. Students who contribute patches or documentation to real projects gain recognition and experience, and they develop a sense of reciprocity toward the community that supports their own work.

Laboratories offer a practical setting for these skills. Students can scan a project for dependencies and licenses, simulate a compliance review, build and publish a small library with proper documentation, and review one another's contributions. They can also practice responding to vulnerability reports in a controlled scenario. Such exercises turn abstract legal and community concepts into habits that employers value highly.

In conclusion, the strength of the software world rests on shared libraries and the licenses that govern them. Using them wisely requires legal awareness, technical judgment, and a willingness to support the people who maintain them. With rigorous teaching, an entrepreneurial culture, and capable laboratories, learners can become both skilled users and generous stewards of open source.
